# The Neighbours

Usenet is one answer to "how do people
talk in writing, in groups, across
machines?" — and several other answers
live next door to it. These are
characterisations from the neighbouring
projects' own documentation and plain
observation, not benchmarks; the lab
ran Usenet only.

## Mailing lists: the same shape, one host

A mailing list is Usenet's closest
relative. The messages are the same
animal — headers, plain text, threading
by `References:` — but delivery runs
through one list server, which mails
each post to every subscriber. The
differences all flow from that host: a
list has an owner who can set rules,
remove members and keep an archive;
joining means your address is on file;
and the conversation lives exactly as
long as the host does. Usenet traded
that manageability away for copies
everywhere and no owner at all. When a
group wants a gardener, it becomes a
mailing list; when it wants to outlive
every gardener, it becomes a newsgroup.

## Web forums: the centre returns

A forum is a newsgroup's topics and
threads rebuilt inside one website —
one database, one account, one
operator. In exchange for the centre,
forums give what Usenet structurally
cannot: editing your post after the
fact, deleting it, moderators with
real power, and search that covers
every word ever posted. The price is
the centre itself: the archive is one
company's asset, export is a courtesy,
and when the site closes the
conversation's whole history closes
with it. Much of the discussion that
once filled text groups now lives in
forums, for exactly these reasons —
chapter 10 is honest about that.

## Reddit: groups as a product

Reddit's subreddits are the newsgroup
idea wearing a front page: named topic
communities, threaded replies, local
moderators. Two additions changed the
flavour: votes, which sort the
conversation by applause, and a single
company underneath it all, whose
account you hold and whose decisions
are final. Usenet has no votes and no
underneath. Old hands who find Reddit
noisy and Usenet quiet are measuring
the same thing from two ends.

## RSS: the one-way neighbour

RSS (and its reader programs — this
series has a
[FreshRSS guide](https://paulsullivan33-dev.github.io/freshrss-for-mere-mortals/))
solves the opposite problem: not
conversation but *announcement*. A
publisher emits items; subscribers'
readers fetch them; there is no reply
path at all. Where a newsgroup is a
town square, an RSS feed is a notice
pinned up by one author. The two are
complements — many a group's periodic
announcements would be a feed today.

## Chat (Matrix, IRC): talk in the present tense

Chat networks — IRC, and Matrix, whose
homeserver has
[its own guide](https://paulsullivan33-dev.github.io/matrix-synapse-for-mere-mortals/)
in this series — are conversation at
speaking speed: rooms, presence,
messages that scroll away. Usenet is
conversation at letter speed: nobody
is "online", articles wait patiently
on your server, and a reply can be
written three days later to a thread
that is still exactly where it was.
Choose chat for a room that's awake;
choose news for a topic that keeps.

## The downloading toolchain

SABnzbd and the *arr tools are not
rivals but Usenet's own downstream:
SABnzbd spends NZBs against a provider
(chapter 6), and Sonarr and Radarr keep
score of a media library that Usenet
helps fill. Both have guides in this
series —
[SABnzbd](https://paulsullivan33-dev.github.io/sabnzbd-for-mere-mortals/)
and
[Sonarr and Radarr](https://paulsullivan33-dev.github.io/arr-stack-for-mere-mortals/) —
where the work this guide only maps is
done live, PAR2 repairs included.
