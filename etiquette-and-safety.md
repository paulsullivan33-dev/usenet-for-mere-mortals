# Etiquette and Safety

The outcome: you know the customs that
keep a discussion group readable — and,
more important, the three safety facts
that cannot be undone once learned the
hard way. The customs are conventions;
the facts are structural, and chapter 1
promised them early.

## Fact one: a post is forever

Your article is copied server to server,
and servers are run by strangers, some
of whom archive everything. A **cancel
message** — a control article asking
servers to delete one of yours — exists,
but honouring it is voluntary, forged
cancels were historically common, and
many servers ignore cancels entirely.
Search engines and private archives are
beyond the protocol altogether.

So the rule is not "be careful with the
cancel button". It is: **post nothing
you would not sign and pin to a public
noticeboard.** Names, addresses, your
employer, your whereabouts, opinions
you would not defend in five years —
assume all of it persists, attributed,
forever. People have lost jobs to posts
they believed were jokes among friends
in a small group. The group was never
small. It was the network.

## Fact two: your From line is public

Every article carries your `From:`
header to every server and every
archive. An address printed in ten
thousand articles will be harvested by
spammers — this was one of the first
mass sources of spam addresses, and it
still works. Old hands used a working
address munged in an obvious way, or a
dedicated account for posting. What you
may not do is use *someone else's*
address, or a fake one that might be
someone's: replies and abuse reports
then land on an innocent stranger.
Many providers also require the address
on your posts to be the one they issued
or registered to you; their terms are
the authority for your account.

## Fact three: files from strangers are untrusted

A binary group's articles were posted
by someone, somewhere, under a name
they chose. Nothing in the machinery
checks that a file is what its subject
line claims, that it is lawful to
share, or that it is safe to run.
Downloaders verify *completeness* —
CRCs, PAR2 — and completeness says
nothing about content. Treat executables
from binary groups as you would a
program handed to you on an unlabelled
disc by a stranger: don't run them, and
if you must examine them, do it nowhere
near anything you value. What is lawful
to download where you live is your own
legal question; a provider account is
not an answer to it, and this guide is
not legal advice — only a clear
statement that the risk is real and is
yours.

## The customs

Against that background, the manners
are almost gentle:

- **Quote sparingly, answer below.**
  Include only the lines you are
  answering, above your reply — the
  lab's seeded thread (t01) shows the
  shape: a two-line quote, then the
  answer. Quoting a whole article to add
  "me too" buries the group in copies.
- **Write subjects that describe.**
  Threads live and die by their subject
  lines; "help" helps nobody search.
  When a thread changes topic, change
  the subject — the `References:` header
  keeps the family together regardless.
- **Cross-post, don't multipost.**
  One article naming two groups (t04)
  is one conversation in two rooms.
  Posting the same text separately to
  each group starts two conversations,
  doubles the storage on every server,
  and annoys both rooms. Cross-post to
  few groups, and only where the topic
  honestly fits both.
- **Test in test groups.** Every network
  has them — the lab's `lab.test` is the
  miniature — and they exist so your
  first-post experiments annoy nobody.
  Posting tests to a real discussion
  group is the classic newcomer's mark.
- **No binaries in discussion groups,**
  no advertising, no chain letters.
  Charters (chapter 5) say what belongs;
  read the group's before posting.
- **Plain text, wrapped lines.** Articles
  are read in everything from a phone
  app to a terminal. Keep lines under
  about 72 characters and skip the
  formatting flourishes that only one
  brand of reader displays.

**Check yourself.** Before your first
real post, three questions: Would I sign
this, publicly, in five years? Is the
address in my `From:` one I chose for
public use? Am I posting it once, to
the right groups, from the test group's
lessons rather than to it? Three yeses
and you are safer than most of the
network's history.
